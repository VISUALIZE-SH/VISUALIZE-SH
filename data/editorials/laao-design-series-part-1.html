Device architectures and the allocation of four functions

Editorial series: Atrial appendage occlusion: four functions, many designs. Part 1 of 4.

Summary. Left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) devices have had their highs and lows in the last few years. However their adoption may be, they are an interesting implant class in terms of their designs. In this Editorial series, we will look at the evolution of LAAO designs and the connections between their functions, mechanical performance, and the design elements. This part reviews LAAO, appendage anatomy, and the development of the device class. It compares how implants allocate four functions: deliver, seal, hold, and heal. Durability is a requirement across all four. The review uses regulatory documents, patents, registries, and published reports available through September 2026. Every endocardial LAAO implant approved in the United States and Japan belongs to the cage or lobe-and-disc family. The architectural comparisons are the author's interpretations, and evidence for newer designs remains limited.

Introduction

Left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO), also called closure, closes a small pouch of the left atrium to reduce stroke risk in atrial fibrillation (AF). Catheter-delivered implants reach the appendage from a vein in the groin [1]. The cage illustrated in Figure 1 expands in the appendage neck near its opening and remains in place after release [2]. This series examines four implant functions: deliver, seal, hold, and heal. It asks what public evidence establishes about their design.

AF is a rhythm disorder of the atria, the upper chambers of the heart, and is associated with stroke. An estimated 59.7 million people had AF or atrial flutter globally in 2019 [3]. A 2016 meta-analysis of 104 cohorts reported a relative risk of stroke of 2.42 with AF [4]. A thrombus (blood clot) leaving the heart can block a cerebral artery and cause a stroke. In AF, left atrial thrombus is often found in the appendage. A 1996 review of 23 studies identified 222 patients with nonrheumatic AF, meaning AF without rheumatic heart disease, and documented left atrial thrombus [5]. In 201 patients (91 percent), thrombus was isolated to, or originated in, the appendage [5].

Oral anticoagulants reduce blood clotting and lower stroke risk [6]. They also carry a risk of major bleeding [7], and patients do not always continue direct oral anticoagulants [8]. These limitations provide the clinical context for appendage closure.1

The 2023 US guideline dates the first report of surgical appendage occlusion to 1949 [9]. Its purpose was to prevent recurrent arterial emboli, clots carried in the blood. Surgical closure during heart surgery is added to anticoagulation [9]. Catheter-based closure began in 2001 [10] and offers some patients with AF an alternative to long-term anticoagulation. WATCHMAN was the first implant approved in the United States. Its sponsor reports that more than 600,000 people have been treated with it [11].

Randomized trials evaluate closure against anticoagulants or other medical care using different composite endpoints (Table 1).2 Most randomized trials of catheter-based closure against anticoagulants met their noninferiority criteria. Noninferiority means that closure was not worse than the comparator by more than a prespecified margin for the tested endpoint. These endpoints combined outcomes such as stroke, bleeding, and death. CLOSURE-AF compared closure with best medical care in patients at high risk of stroke and bleeding; closure was not noninferior [12]. LAAOS III tested a different intervention: surgical occlusion added to usual care, including anticoagulation, during cardiac surgery. Stroke or systemic embolism occurred in 4.8 percent with occlusion versus 7.0 percent with usual care alone [13]. The 2023 US guideline considers catheter-based LAAO reasonable when long-term anticoagulation is contraindicated for a nonreversible reason [9]. The 2024 European guideline states that it may be considered (class IIb) when long-term anticoagulation is contraindicated [14].

The appendage and the implant procedure

The appendage usually arises from the anterolateral, or front and side, wall of the left atrium (Figure 1) [30]. Three regions describe its geometry: the orifice opening into the atrium, the neck containing the implant landing zone, and the lobed body [30]. The orifice is usually oval [30, 31], and the body varies between patients. In 932 patients with drug-refractory AF scheduled for ablation, appendages were classified as chicken wing, cactus, windsock, or cauliflower [32]. Their respective proportions were 48, 30, 19, and 3 percent [32]. These classes depend partly on imaging direction [30]. The mean lobar wall thickness was 0.7 mm in 250 autopsy hearts [33]. A ridge separates the orifice from the left superior pulmonary vein [30]. The mitral valve and circumflex coronary artery lie close to the orifice [30, 31].

Catheter-delivered implants reach the left atrium from the femoral vein through the interatrial septum, the wall between the atria [1]. The puncture is made at the fossa ovalis, an oval depression in the septum (Figure 1a) [1]. Echocardiography, ultrasound imaging of the heart, and fluoroscopy, real-time X-ray imaging, guide deployment [2, 34]. The implant expands from a sheath advanced into the appendage (Figure 1c) [2]. Before release, the WATCHMAN FLX label requires checks of position, anchoring, size, and seal [2]. Follow-up echocardiography or computed tomography (CT) at about 45 days checks for leak and thrombus on the implant [2, 34].

Appendage geometry sets several requirements that the implant must meet together. It must seal an orifice that is usually oval and changes shape after implantation (Part 3). Fixation must avoid injury to a thin wall: mean thicknesses were 0.7 mm in the lobes and 1.0 to 1.9 mm in the neck [33]. Nearby structures, including the circumflex artery and mitral valve, also constrain placement. The pulmonary artery can lie directly against the appendage. On electrocardiogram-gated CT in 55 patients, proximal appendage and pulmonary artery lumens were less than 1 mm apart in 8 [39]. The atrial face is intended to become covered by tissue. The device descriptions list delivery catheters or sheaths in French sizes (Table 2); 12 to 14 Fr corresponds to a nominal diameter of about 4 to 4.7 mm. Catheter and sheath dimensions describe different components and are not interchangeable measures of access profile. The implants must also remain intact. FDA summaries for WATCHMAN and Amulet report fatigue tests to 400 million cycles, described as ten years of use [38, 40, 41] (Part 4).

Four functions and one requirement

Figure 1c identifies the components involved in the four functions. Clinical observations provide incomplete measures of each function:

  • Deliver (deployment). Pass through a catheter, expand in the appendage, and permit repositioning before release. Recapture draws a deployed implant back into its sheath for another attempt. Implant success and procedural events are the clinical proxies.
  • Seal (occlusion). Close the orifice to prevent blood exchange between the appendage and atrium. In the generic cage, the fabric face and contact at the device perimeter provide the barrier. Residual flow on imaging is the clinical proxy. Peridevice leak is flow around the implant's edge.
  • Hold (fixation). Maintain position without injuring the wall. Anchors engage the tissue, and frame contact also contributes to fixation. Device embolization, detachment into the bloodstream, is the proxy for loss of fixation. Wall injury appears as perforation, erosion, or pericardial effusion, fluid in the sac around the heart. Effusion can also result from delivery [42].
  • Heal (tissue coverage). Support tissue coverage of the atrial face and hub after implantation. Device-related thrombus (DRT), thrombus on the atrial face, is the clinical proxy. DRT also reflects placement and the drug regimen, so it is not a direct measure of tissue coverage (Part 2).

Durability is a requirement across all four functions. Components must remain intact during delivery, permitted recapture, subsequent cardiac motion, and exposure to blood. It is therefore assessed throughout the series rather than treated as a separate function.

Functions interact when they share a component or depend on the same tissue. Anchors added for fixation change where the wall is loaded. Recapture bends those anchors into the sheath, while sealing by oversizing stretches the surrounding tissue. These interactions connect architecture to the design changes, sizing rules, and durability evidence examined in the later parts.

Allocation means assigning a function to a component or feature, such as the frame, fabric, anchors, or appendage tissue. Coupling occurs when changing a feature for one function also changes another. Separation allows features to be adjusted independently, but usually leaves a dependency elsewhere. Coupling can be beneficial or harmful. A clinical review discusses an uncovered ridge beside the left superior pulmonary vein as a site for device-related thrombus [43]. This provides clinical context for examining the seal boundary and healing surface together, although it does not establish that one causes the other.

Four questions organize the comparison of architectures and design changes:

  1. Which feature becomes independently adjustable?
  2. Which sensitivity is reduced?
  3. Which dependency remains?
  4. What evidence would show whether the change helped?

Sensitivity means the dependence of performance on an anatomical or procedural variable, such as the dependence of fixation on precise placement. The final question concerns both device performance and the extent to which public evidence can establish it.

Development of the device class, 2001–2026

LAAO devices developed in lineages, sequences of versions sharing an origin (Figure 2). PLAATO (percutaneous left atrial appendage transcatheter occlusion), the first catheter-delivered device, combined the four functions in a nitinol cage. Expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) covered the cage, and small anchors lay along its struts [44, 45]. Nitinol is a nickel–titanium alloy that recovers its shape after large deformation. It became the frame material of nearly every later device. PLAATO's owner, ev3, ended the program in 2005 because the trial FDA required was "not commercially feasible" [46].

WATCHMAN also combined several functions in one frame. Its developers describe a self-expanding structure modeled on a stent, with early iterations guided by bench and preclinical testing [47]. They chose an open distal end, deeper in the appendage, and polyethylene terephthalate (PET), a polyester fabric, rather than a closed end and polytetrafluoroethylene [47]. They attribute this choice to intellectual-property considerations [47]. They also report that these features later accommodated more anatomies and promoted faster healing [47].

The first documented change concerned holding. Two of the first 13 implants embolized, and the investigators describe the next device as carrying "a series of barbs to facilitate device attachment and prevent embolization" [48]. No device embolized in the 53 patients implanted afterward [48].

In October 2006, during PROTECT AF, a WATCHMAN implant 20 percent shorter at each diameter entered the trial [49]. PROTECT AF was submitted to support approval. The sponsor stated that shortening was intended "to minimize length requirements" [49]. FDA described it as part of an iterative redesign also intended to reduce pericardial perforation and effusion [50]. Two recaptured shorter implants were subsequently found with missing barbs [50]. Use was suspended, the barb was redesigned, and the implant returned to the trial [50].

The sequence connects an anatomical design change with a delivery load on fixation features. Shortening reduced the required depth and, in FDA's account, was intended to reduce perforation and effusion. Recapture bends the barbs into the sheath, imposing a different load from cardiac motion. The damage was observed after recapture, but the documents do not establish its cause [50]. The episode therefore illustrates shared components and load paths without identifying shortening as the cause of barb damage.

A second lineage began in Bern in June 2002, when Bernhard Meier placed an atrial septal occluder in a left atrial appendage [51]. He recalls an acute device embolization rate close to 10 percent as the reason the approach was abandoned [51]. Its purpose-built successor, the Amplatzer Cardiac Plug (ACP), separated the functions between a lobe carrying anchors and a disc covering the orifice, an arrangement Meier calls the "pacifier principle" [51]. The ACP received the CE mark, the European conformity marking, in December 2008 [52].

FDA approved WATCHMAN 2.5 in March 2015 [40]. The approved version was the shorter implant with the redesigned barb [40, 50]. WATCHMAN FLX received US approval in July 2020, and Amulet in August 2021, both without a panel [38, 41]. Table 2 gives the European and Japanese dates. The notes distinguish regulatory accounts from differing descriptions in published reviews.3

Amulet, the successor to the ACP, received the CE mark in 2013. Other lineages followed: WaveCrest (CE mark 2013), LAmbre and the original Occlutech LAA occluder (2016), and Ultraseal, also in 2016 according to its investigators (Table 2). Devices that close the appendage from outside the heart developed in parallel: the LARIAT suture loop and surgical clips (Table 2). The LARIAT was cleared in the United States from 2006 for soft-tissue approximation, not for appendage closure. Figure 2 shows the status of each lineage in September 2026.4

Cage and lobe-and-disc architectures

Cage architecture. WATCHMAN 2.5 is a laser-cut, electropolished nitinol umbrella frame (Figure 3a) [40]. A knit fabric covers its proximal, atrial face [40]. Fixation anchors lie on the struts' outer edges, and a threaded hub connects the implant to its delivery system [40]. WATCHMAN FLX (US 2020) is closed at both ends, has 18 J-shaped anchors in two rows [53], and permits full recapture [38].

In a single-frame device, some features can be changed independently, while others share a load path. The fabric, surface treatment, and hub can in principle be changed without altering the anchors. An integral anchor cut from a strut junction provides a useful conceptual example: if that junction acts as a flexural hinge, compressing the frame can change both anchor orientation and radial force. This is a mechanical interpretation of a shared load path, rather than a statement about a particular commercial frame's detailed construction. Radial force is the force between implant and wall during expansion or compression. The sealing fabric is supported by the same struts. Stiffening a hinge for greater outward force can therefore change strain at the anchor root and tension in the fabric.

Lobe-and-disc architecture. Amulet (US 2021) has a braided nitinol lobe and disc joined by a waist, with polyester fabric in both [41]. Radial force and stabilizing wires provide fixation at the lobe. These wires, Amulet's anchors, are sewn to it with polyester thread [54]. The disc seals at the orifice (Figure 3b).

The lobe and disc separate fixation from sealing by location, and attached wires allow anchor stiffness to be set separately from the body [55]. Separation remains incomplete because disc diameter follows the selected lobe diameter [41], and the lobe also seals. Lobe position sets the waist tension that draws the disc onto the orifice. Braid stiffness depends on wire count and diameter, braid angle, heat-set shape, and end constraints. Lobe compression lengthens the braid, moving the waist and changing how the wires meet the wall. Each attached wire also introduces a joint into the fixation load path.

Anchors differ on two axes. By construction, an anchor is integral with the load-bearing body, attached to it through a joint such as a suture or eyelet, or carried on a separate element. By deployment, an anchor is passive, engaging as the implant expands, or actuated, set after the seal has been checked. An anchor cut from the frame is integral; an anchor that engages as the frame expands is passive. Amulet's sewn wires are attached and passive, whereas the anchors of WaveCrest and Zenith are actuated.

Architecture and fabrication are separate choices. A cut tube favors integral features, and a braid favors a closed, recapturable body. The pairing is not fixed, however, because Zenith is braided with a separate anchor array [56] and WaveCrest is laser-cut with actuated anchors [57].

The cage and the lobe and disc remain the reference designs. In the United States and Japan, every approved endocardial LAAO device (one placed inside the heart) belongs to one of the two families. Every device-versus-device trial located for this series used one of them as its comparator.5 In SWISS-APERO, which randomized Amulet against WATCHMAN devices, contrast agent crossed through the Amulet more often, and peridevice leak on echocardiography was more common with WATCHMAN [58].6

Alternative allocations of the four functions

Other designs assign functions to a deployment step, compliant foam, an adhesive, or appendage tissue. They represent alternative allocations rather than a single chronological stage of development. The Sideris patch, for example, was reported in 2011, before Amulet's first implant.

Adhesive fixation. The Sideris device was a frameless polyurethane-foam patch delivered on a balloon. A pH-activated adhesive held it and required 45 minutes to set [59]. The patch succeeded in 17 of 20 patients [59]. All three non-adhering patches occurred in patients who underwent angiography, X-ray imaging with contrast agent, during the waiting period [59]. The inventors proposed a successor that would fix immediately by expansion [59]. Fixation in the tested design depended on adhesion chemistry and setting time.

Staged fixation. WaveCrest allows sealing to be checked before fixation. Its laser-cut frame carries an ePTFE atrial face and polyurethane foam [57, 60]. After placement and a seal check, 20 anchors are deployed from the hub [57, 60]. Fixation therefore depends partly on a mechanism and procedural step. Zenith applies the same principle using a separate anchor array. Its polyester-covered braided scaffold seals, and eight anchors are deployed after the seal is confirmed [56]. This sequence separates positioning from fixation while retaining a dependency on anchor deployment.

Compliant sealing. Investigational CLAAS combines a flexible nitinol endoskeleton with a conformable foam cup and an ePTFE atrial face. Its Regular and Large implants are nominally 27 and 35 mm [61]. In a silicone-tube experiment, compression along the minor axis allowed the device to expand along the major axis and maintain contact with an oval opening [62]. This suggests an engineering advantage: compliance in the frame-and-foam assembly may let two sizes accommodate a broad range of shapes, reducing the number of discrete sizes needed. That interpretation concerns adaptability, not proven clinical superiority or elimination of sizing requirements.

The investigational IFU's combined ostial-diameter envelope spans 10–40 mm, with separate conditions for each size. For Regular, both minimum and maximum diameters must lie within 10–33 mm and their mean must be no greater than 25 mm; for Large, both must lie within 20–40 mm and their mean must be no greater than 32 mm. Both require at least 10 mm landing-zone depth [61]. Thus 10–40 mm is not a mean-diameter range or permission to treat every opening within it.7 Early clinical failures included undersizing when only one size was available [63]. Compliance changes the sizing problem, but does not remove it.8

Tissue-based sealing and fixation. LAMINAR advances a nitinol ball into the appendage and rotates it about 360 degrees. Tissue wraps around the spindle, and a lock compresses the wrapped tissue [64]. The first report describes no fabric, side hooks, or barbs [64]. Sealing and fixation depend on tissue remaining wrapped and on the lock. The pivotal trial was suspended in 2025; its registry cites "potential unwrapping of the LAA [left atrial appendage] post implant with clinically significant leaks" [65].9

Closure from outside the heart. The epicardial LARIAT suture loop and surgical clips close the appendage from outside the heart, so no implant is left in the bloodstream. The surface left to heal is mainly the stump. Reports about the LARIAT and the TigerPaw II fastener include tissue events such as perforation, detachment of the appendage, stump thrombus, and tearing.10

Dependencies and reported events. Alternative allocations permit a seal check before fixation, change seal compliance, or avoid an implant on the atrial face. They also retain dependencies on deployment, adhesion and setting time, foam sizing, or tissue integrity. Reported events include non-adhesion, undersizing, potential unwrapping, detachment, and tearing. These events identify relevant failure modes, but do not establish comparative superiority among architectures.

Limits of comparative evidence. Evidence for these designs consists mainly of small series, case reports, registry records, and safety communications. WAVECREST 2 compared WaveCrest with WATCHMAN and closed for "device design modification", wording added to the registry after the study ended [66]. Posted results from its small randomized arms were mixed [66]. Figure 4 compares allocations across eight architectures, and Table 2 lists the allocations by device.

Summary of findings

  1. In the United States and Japan, every approved endocardial LAAO device is either a cage or a lobe and disc.
  2. In the 2006 WATCHMAN episode, a change made for one function coincided with damage to a feature that serves another, in a single frame. The documents give no cause for the barb damage.
  3. No architecture reviewed makes the four functions independent of one another. Each leaves dependencies at identifiable places: a cage's hinges, a lobe-and-disc's waist and wire joints, and a separated design's deployment step or tissue. These are the places to examine when a design changes.
  4. Anchors differ by construction (integral, attached, or separate) and by deployment (passive or actuated).
  5. Architecture and fabrication are separate choices.
  6. Evidence for the designs that separate functions further is limited.

Part 2 examines changes to anchors, ends, hubs, and surfaces within these architectures. It separates the documented reasons for each change from the outcomes that can be attributed to it.
