Design changes between device generations and their documented outcomes

Editorial series: Atrial appendage occlusion: four functions, many designs. Part 2 of 4.

Summary. Sponsors have changed anchors, ends, hubs, and surfaces between generations of left atrial appendage occluders. This part compares the stated reasons for those changes with their documented outcomes. Amulet 360 is one of the few generations for which a regulatory summary gives a reason for each change. Where outcomes were measured, some generations recorded better outcomes than their predecessors. The available documents do not identify which changes produced those outcomes.

In this series

  1. Device architectures and the allocation of four functions
  2. Design changes between device generations and their documented outcomes
  3. Sizing, compression and radial force
  4. Durability: testing, analysis and field experience

Introduction

Left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) closes the left atrial appendage, a small pouch of the left atrium where thrombus (blood clot) is often found in patients with atrial fibrillation. It can serve as an alternative to long-term anticoagulation for stroke prevention in some patients (Part 1). Most implants are self-expanding frames of nitinol, a nickel–titanium alloy, delivered by catheter. This series describes four implant functions: deliver, seal, hold, and heal. Durability is a requirement across all four. Allocation assigns each function to a component; coupling means that a change for one function also affects another. The analysis asks four questions about each change:

  1. Which feature becomes independently adjustable?
  2. Which sensitivity is reduced?
  3. Which dependency remains?
  4. What evidence would show whether the change helped?

This part applies those questions first to Amulet 360, then examines the early WATCHMAN FLX redesign, changes to holding and healing, and the limits of attributing outcomes to design.

Amulet 360 design rationale and outcomes

In the US pivotal trial, patients were randomized to Amulet, a braided nitinol lobe-and-disc device, or WATCHMAN 2.5, a laser-cut cage. Amulet had a higher rate of appendage seal, defined as a leak of 5 mm or less at 45 days (98.9 versus 96.8 percent) [1, 2]. Pericardial effusion, fluid in the sac around the heart, required treatment more often with Amulet (3.2 versus 1.5 percent) [1].

The sponsor describes the next generation, Amulet 360, as having "design enhancements intended to strengthen safety while maintaining effectiveness" [3]. The Amulet 360 study report names pericardial effusion among the problems of current devices [4]. The European summary of safety and clinical performance (SSCP) for Amulet 360 lists four changes, each with a reason [3]:

  • distal end screw removed, "to improve lobe conformability and ease of use during device positioning";
  • distal lobe edge rounded, to improve conformability "and aid in consistent stabilizing anchor protrusion";
  • anchors given a "modified geometry ... to allow for full recapture" (recapture is drawing a deployed implant back into its sheath before release, for another attempt) and fixed by eyelets;
  • second row of anchors added, "to achieve sufficient anchoring in instances of less precise lobe deployment".

The Amulet 360 documents address more of the four questions than those for most generations reviewed. The design features changed were the anchors and distal lobe end. The sponsor aimed to reduce anchoring's dependence on precise lobe deployment. Dependencies remain during deployment and after release. Every anchor must pass through the sheath during recapture. The second row adds wall contact points, and the waist still joins the lobe to the disc.

The study provides only part of the evidence needed to answer the fourth question. Among 400 patients, it recorded no device embolization, or detachment of the implant into the bloodstream [3, 4]. At 45 days, two patients (0.5 percent) required drainage for pericardial effusion; this follow-up window was shorter than in the pivotal trial [3, 4]. Of 376 patients with an evaluable echocardiogram, 93.9 percent had no leak at 45 days [3, 4]. The corresponding no-residual-jet proportion in the original Amulet randomized IDE cohort was 63.0 percent at 45 days [1, 2]. This is distinct from the 98.9 percent endpoint allowing a residual jet of up to 5 mm. The two no-leak proportions describe separate studies with different populations and procedural contexts; their difference cannot establish an effect of the 360 design changes. These results are consistent with the stated aim, but the single-arm study does not provide a comparison. It was assessed against performance goals, or preset thresholds derived from earlier Amulet data [3]. The SSCP therefore states that these outcomes “cannot be directly compared” with those for the predicate device, the original Amulet [3].1 The study also cannot identify which change produced the observed results.

One review proposes that hooks, radial force (the force between the implant and the wall), a stiffer lobe, and disc tension probably explain Amulet's higher effusion rate [5]. The rounded distal lobe edge of Amulet 360 is consistent with that hypothesis. The device also adds a second row of anchors, and no reviewed document tests the hypothesis.

Early WATCHMAN FLX redesign and outcomes

The WATCHMAN sequence illustrates a more common documentation gap. Boston Scientific sold the early FLX in a limited European release from November 2015 to March 2016 [6, 7]. WATCHMAN 2.5 has one row of 10 anchors [6]. The early FLX instead had 18 struts, a closed distal end, and 12 J-shaped anchors in two rows, which investigators said aided “device stabilization” [6]. The sponsor suspended the release “due to a higher than expected rate of device embolization” [8] and “elected to make design enhancements” [7].2 Investigators described the redesign's main aim as “improving the overall device stability to allow for a more forgiving placement at the ostium” [6]. The later FLX has 18 J-shaped anchors, nine in each row [9, 10]. Anchor count is the only documented change between the early and later FLX, but no reviewed document identifies it or another feature as the remedy.

In PINNACLE FLX, the single-arm trial supporting US approval, no device embolized among 400 patients [11]. Equal-sized US registry cohorts recorded fewer in-hospital major adverse events with FLX than with WATCHMAN 2.5 (1.35 versus 2.40 percent) [12]. They also recorded less effusion requiring intervention (0.42 versus 1.23 percent) and device embolization (0.02 versus 0.06 percent) [12]. A follow-up comparison recorded less peridevice leak, flow around the implant edge, at 45 days (14.7 versus 23.5 percent) [13]. Ischemic stroke was also less frequent at one year (adjusted hazard ratio 0.82) [13]. These observational comparisons favor FLX on each reported measure, although the cohorts differ in era and discharge drugs.3

The FLX generation changed struts, ends, anchors, height, and recapture together. Its trial protocol lists design objectives relative to WATCHMAN 2.5, including a closed distal end, full recapture, more contact points, and less exposed metal [7]. The protocol does not identify which change addressed the early FLX embolizations. Investigators suggest that lower height, two anchor rows, and full recapture may have contributed to the absence of embolization in the FLX trial [10]. Although the generation recorded better outcomes, public documents do not attribute them to a specific feature; the changes were introduced together.

Features for holding and injury to the wall

Features that hold an implant also affect where and how it loads the wall. This matters because the appendage wall is thin [14], and the pulmonary artery can lie in direct contact with it (Part 1) [15]. A 2013 case report described a WATCHMAN of unstated generation whose strut protruded through the wall and eroded the pulmonary artery [16]. The authors suggested a tip “rounded rather than pointed” [16]. An Abbott adverse-event report to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) states that Amulet's stabilizing wires, or anchors, can pass through the wall into the pulmonary artery. The report names poor positioning, oversizing, and a nearby or enlarged artery as possible factors [17]. In a Brazilian series, LAmbre anchoring hooks perforated the main pulmonary artery in 1 of 51 patients [18].

Holding by attached wires in lobe-and-disc devices. A stabilizing wire is an anchoring wire attached around the lobe that engages the appendage wall to resist device displacement [1, 19]. The Amplatzer Cardiac Plug (ACP) became the ACP 2, later called Amulet, with a thicker stabilizing wire, a longer lobe, and additional wire pairs at some sizes [20].4 Investigators write that most ACP embolizations “have been linked to an inadequate implantation depth” [20]. They also warn that more and stiffer stabilizing wires may damage the appendage during aggressive handling [20]. FDA later reported that five of six Amulet pivotal-trial embolizations occurred early in the implanter's experience and “might be related to suboptimal device sizing” [1]. A 2026 notice for Amulet, not Amulet 360, added label guidance on the distance between the landing zone in the appendage neck and the pulmonary artery [21, 22].

Anchoring changes in other device lines. Occlutech's original device used atraumatic loops to hold it. After four dislocations in 2016, the sponsor first pointed to operator training and guidance [23].5 The later LAA Plus, tested in 12 pigs, adds five to eight hooks because of “insufficient anchoring” [24]. No human use is documented, and no reviewed document links the dislocations to the Plus. In Lifetech's line, a canine study identified hook penetration as the main future safety concern [25]; the later LAxible has blunted barb tips [26].

Anchor loads during recapture. Recapture bends curved anchors toward the sheath, imposing a loading path different from cardiac motion. Although nitinol is superelastic, repeated large deformations can leave residual plastic strain; experiments on processed NiTi wires show that the response depends on microstructure and temperature [27]. For an anchor, excessive local strain during repeated resheathing could therefore require a change in geometry or processing to limit permanent deformation and fracture risk. This is a design possibility, not a demonstrated mechanism for a particular LAAO failure.

FDA recommends that nitinol assessment account for implantation deformation, clinically relevant temperature, and the effect of prestrain on fatigue [28]. As described in Part 1, WATCHMAN barbs were found damaged after recapture in 2006, although the documents give no cause [29].6 Bench testing found that repeated resheathing reduced WATCHMAN's diameter by up to 7.9 percent and lowered its radial force [30]. Amulet 360's anchors were reshaped “to allow for full recapture” [3]. The observed diameter and force changes do not identify the deformation mechanism at an anchor. Recapture serves delivery, but it loads the anchors that serve holding.

Holding and wall injury. The effect of a holding feature on wall injury depends on its geometry, placement, handling, and the patient's anatomy. Anchors apply load at discrete points, whereas radial force spreads the load. The reports above identify positioning and sizing as well as struts and wires as relevant factors. Registry comparisons recorded less device embolization and effusion with FLX than with WATCHMAN 2.5. Amulet 360 added a row of anchors and recorded no embolization in its single-arm study. Neither result identifies the effect of the anchors. Fixation features therefore require evaluation of both retention and wall injury.

Healing surfaces and attachment sites

Before tissue covers an implant, thrombus can form on its atrial face. This thrombus is called device-related thrombus (DRT). Designers have repeatedly changed exposed metal and attachment sites that they consider likely starting points. Amulet's disc screw was recessed [20], and FLX covers a low-profile insert with fabric “to reduce exposed metal” [10].7 FLX Pro changed the surface chemistry by adding a polymer coating to the FLX platform [31, 32].8

Dependencies of the healing surface. The healing surface is the atrial face of the sealing component. At its center, the hub or screw connects the implant to the delivery cable. Designers in several lineages have recessed, covered, or removed this attachment site. At the rim, the healing surface also forms the edge of the seal. The implant's contact with the ridge beside the pulmonary vein therefore affects both healing and sealing.

The reported thrombus locations are consistent with this account of the healing surface. In a multicenter cohort, mostly involving WATCHMAN implants, 55.3 percent of DRT sites were on the disc or atrial face, and another 12.7 percent were at the screw or hub [33]. In the Amulet 360 study, three of nine thrombi were at a central screw; the 360 removed only the distal screw. Five were at the edge near the pulmonary-vein ridge [4]. Pooled lobe-and-disc data relayed by a review also associate thrombus with an uncovered ridge (Part 1) [34]. Implant placement and drug regimens provide other explanations. Investigators attributed ACP thrombus to the protruding pin [20], while the manufacturer's 2011 notice named implant depth as the most probable cause [35].9

Evidence by study type. The studies differ in the claims they can support.

  • An animal study can test a proposed mechanism. In the sponsor's canine study, the coated FLX Pro surface had less thrombus at two and four weeks [36].10
  • A single-arm study reports a rate but cannot provide a comparison. HEAL-LAA reported DRT in 1.6 percent of FLX Pro patients at 45 days and 2.7 percent at 12 months; its authors described these rates as “similar to the predecessor device” [37].
  • A comparison between generations can show an association, but not its cause. In the US registry, DRT was 0.4 percent with FLX and 0.5 percent with WATCHMAN 2.5 [13]. Two two-center cohorts that may overlap found larger differences [38, 39].
  • Each explant, an implant removed from a patient, shows tissue coverage in one patient [40, 41].

No clinical study reviewed isolates the effect of the hub or coating. Designers in six lineages have pursued reductions in exposed metal or changes to attachment sites. Clinical comparisons report favorable findings in some cohorts and no difference in others.

Limits of attributing outcomes to design changes

One clinical review attributes the decline in complications, leak, and DRT over time to device design and implantation technique [42]. Another considers refinements in design, technique, and operator skill likely explanations [5]. The documents reviewed here often cannot separate design effects from other factors:

  1. Operator experience. In PROTECT AF, procedural events fell from 9.9 to 4.8 percent between the first and second halves of enrollment [43]. The sponsor credited the decline to “training, the implant procedure, and technical aspects” of the device; the short WATCHMAN implant also entered the trial partway through [43].11 In US practice, the unchanged Amulet had a 45-day effusion intervention rate of 2.3 percent among operators with fewer than 10 cases [44]. The rate was 1.5 percent among operators with 30 or more cases [44].
  2. Evidence shared between generations. FDA's FLX summary applies animal data from earlier WATCHMAN devices “due to the use of identical materials and substantially similar device designs” [11]. It also states that the FLX trial results “are applicable to” WATCHMAN 2.5 because of their similar designs [11]. The trial's performance goals were derived from WATCHMAN 2.5 data [7]. Bridging evidence across generations is an established regulatory practice and can be sound for many questions. Part 4 examines what “identical materials” establishes.
  3. Drug regimens. The registry cohorts differ in discharge drugs [13]. Since those comparisons, labeled post-implant regimens changed for WATCHMAN in 2022 and 2025 and for Amulet in 2025 [22, 45].
  4. Follow-up and imaging. The Amulet 360 study reported effusion at 45 days, a shorter follow-up window than the pivotal trial [3, 4]. Echocardiography was available for about two thirds of patients in the FLX registry follow-up [13].
  5. Era. The FLX registry cohorts differ in era: WATCHMAN 2.5 patients were treated immediately before each site's first FLX case [12].
  6. Several changes at once. FLX and Amulet 360 each changed several features together. Their outcomes therefore cannot be assigned to a single change.

FDA's 2009 table numbers the implant and its accessories separately; its “Gen 4” refers to a delivery or access system [29]. Amulet catalog numbers still read 9-ACP2 [21].12

Summary of findings

  1. Where outcomes were measured, some generations recorded better outcomes than their predecessors. The public documents do not attribute the FLX registry or Amulet 360 single-arm results to a specific change.
  2. Holding features affect how the wall is loaded, so holding and wall injury may trade off.
  3. Healing is coupled to delivery at the hub and sealing at the rim. Thrombus has been associated with hubs, surfaces, and ridge coverage; implant depth is another reported factor that this interpretation does not explain.
  4. Shared evidence between generations, operator experience, drug regimens, follow-up, imaging, and era limit attribution of outcomes to a single change.
  5. The four questions connect an adjustable feature to its expected outcome and identify other outcomes to check based on dependencies that remain.
  6. The Amulet 360 SSCP gives a reason for each change, showing how regulatory documents can report design rationale. Part 4 examines what additional information they could provide.

The WATCHMAN and Amulet implants described here are sized to their landing zones and released after assessment of their compression. Part 3, Sizing, compression, and radial force, examines what that compression measures in relation to holding and sealing.
