Sizing, compression and radial force

Editorial series: Atrial appendage occlusion: four functions, many designs. Part 3 of 4.

Summary. This part reviews sizing and release rules, reported force data, nitinol mechanics, leak studies and computational models. Sizing rules differ by device and country, and the measured orifice can change after implantation. Compression is a geometric measurement; it does not directly establish wall force, holding or continuous contact around the perimeter. Superelastic nitinol can exert a low outward force while resisting further compression with a higher force. Studies associate leak with compression and implant alignment, but the 2025 compression comparison is observational. No measured force–diameter curves were found for WATCHMAN 2.5, FLX, FLX Pro or Amulet in the public sources reviewed.

Introduction

Left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) closes the left atrial appendage, a small pouch of the left atrium where thrombus (blood clot) is often found in patients with atrial fibrillation. It is used in some patients as an alternative to long-term anticoagulation to prevent stroke (Part 1). Most implants are self-expanding frames of nitinol, a nickel–titanium alloy, delivered by catheter. This series describes four functions: seal, hold, heal and deliver. Durability is a requirement under all four. Allocation assigns each function to a component. Coupling occurs when a change for one function also changes another. Four questions organize the analysis of a design change:

  1. Which feature becomes independently adjustable?
  2. Which sensitivity is reduced?
  3. Which dependency remains?
  4. What evidence would show whether the change helped?

In the devices examined here, one compressed body performs both holding and sealing. Its fit therefore links these functions: a sizing change for one can affect the other. The sizing rules select an implant larger than the measured orifice or landing zone, so the wall compresses the implant. The orifice opens into the left atrium; the landing zone is the neck region where the implant sits. Compression is the reduction in diameter, expressed as a percentage of nominal size (the labeled diameter), at the implant's widest plane on echocardiography or computed tomography (CT). Studies use different reference diameters and imaging methods. At release, compression is used as a geometric indicator of fit. It does not directly measure radial force against the wall or anchoring resistance, and it does not establish continuous perimeter contact. These quantities require separate evidence.

The discussion proceeds from sizing rules and observational leak data to force measurements, nitinol mechanics, sealing, simulation and possible release criteria. It asks whether compression can adequately represent both holding and sealing.

Sizing rules of three devices

WATCHMAN implants are laser-cut nitinol frames, whereas Amulet is a braided nitinol lobe and disc. Their rules share terms but differ in content (Table 1, Figure 1).

The United States (US) instructions for use of WATCHMAN 2.5, approved there in 2015, contain two tables [1]. The first selects the implant from the maximum width of the ostium (the labels' word for the orifice), measured by ultrasound in four views, preferably transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). Each implant size covers orifices 2 to 4 mm narrower than itself. The second, a release check given as a guide, asks that the deployed implant measure 80 to 92 percent of its original diameter at the plane of maximum diameter [1]. The two tables thus refer to the orifice before implant and to the implant after it.1

For WATCHMAN FLX and FLX Pro, the US instructions contain one table and print no percentage [2, 3]. The table's column is headed "Max LAA [left atrial appendage] Ostium Width and/or Deployed Closure Device Diameter". The table's ranges are exactly 70 to 90 percent of nominal size at every size, so the rule is proportional. That is 10 to 30 percent compression. The percentage appears, however, in other published sources [4, 5] and, until September 2025, in Japanese package inserts [6]. The US instructions list four checks before release, the PASS criteria: position, anchor, size (compression) and seal [2].

For Amulet, the US instructions call the checks before release the CLOSE criteria [7]. Their criterion for compression is qualitative: the lobe "should be slightly compressed and have good apposition" (contact) to the wall [7]. The sizing chart sets a fixed oversize over the landing zone, where the lobe sits. The oversize is 3 to 5 mm for smaller lobes and 3 to 6 mm for larger ones [7]. As a fraction of the lobe, that is 19 to 31 percent at 16 mm and 9 to 18 percent at 34 mm.

Proportional and fixed-millimeter rules both specify geometric ranges. Neither establishes how force or perimeter contact varies with implant size, and the public documents do not explain how the tables were derived. Provisional engineering interpretation.

Practice departs from the fixed-millimeter rule. In the US registry, 64.4 percent of 68,456 WATCHMAN 2.5 implants were larger than the selection table recommended [8]. Oversizing was associated with lower odds of a composite of device migration or device embolization (detachment of the implant into the bloodstream) and significant leak, mainly through less leak [8].

Compression beyond 30 percent: one observational study

A 2025 two-center study examined patients with WATCHMAN FLX implants [12]. Compression exceeded 30 percent in 61 patients, above the 10–30 percent range listed in a manufacturer-criteria review and printed in Japanese package inserts until 2025 (Table 1) [4, 6]. At two months, peridevice leak (flow around the edge of the implant) was present in 8.2 percent of these patients and 32.6 percent of the 175 patients with lower compression [12]. Because compression was not assigned, this was an observational comparison. Procedural complications were too few to judge safety.2 The observed difference establishes neither a better compression target nor a mechanism for the difference in leak.

These leak findings matter because peridevice leak has been associated with clinical outcomes. A US registry compared small leaks, up to 5 mm, after WATCHMAN with no leak [13]. Small leaks were associated with modestly more stroke, transient ischemic attack or systemic embolism (adjusted hazard ratio 1.15) [13]. In the Amulet pivotal trial, leaks of 3 mm or more were associated with more stroke, systemic embolism or cardiovascular death (hazard ratio 1.66) [14]. Both findings are associations; the studies used different leak thresholds and outcomes [13, 14].3

Published data on radial force

Radial force describes interaction between implant and wall: the outward force as the implant expands, or its resistance to further compression. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) summaries for WATCHMAN 2.5, FLX and Amulet name force tests and report each result as "Pass". None, however, gives a value, a limit, a fixture, a size or a test temperature [15–17].4

One bench study measured seven occluder systems across their sizing ranges [18]. The force needed to pull an implant out (pull-out force) correlated with the number of anchoring hooks per millimeter of circumference (r = 0.87) [18]. The authors concluded that stability depends more on anchoring structures than on radial force [18].5 A computational model of a 25 mm Amulet lobe gives roughly 1 N of added force per millimeter of diameter reduction [19].6 By contrast, no measured force–diameter curve for WATCHMAN 2.5, FLX, FLX Pro or Amulet was found in public sources.

Relation between compression and radial force

Superelasticity helps explain why compression does not directly measure force. A superelastic alloy recovers large deformations through a reversible change in crystal structure. As nitinol is compressed, it transforms at nearly constant stress along the upper plateau of its loading curve (stress against strain). On release, it transforms back at a lower stress along the lower plateau of its unloading curve. Duerig and colleagues summarized this behavior: "the force applied by a superelastic device is determined by temperature, not strain as in conventional materials" [20].

A self-expanding implant opens as it unloads from the delivery sheath, until it meets the wall. For a superelastic frame operating on its transformation plateau, outward force can remain low while resistance to further compression is much higher. Duerig and colleagues call this response biased stiffness (Figure 2) [20, 21]. Thus, a frame can press lightly on the wall while resisting deformation.

The material plateau alone does not determine an implant's force–diameter curve. Geometry, leverage at hinges and crossings, end constraints, contact and loading history also matter. The plateau nevertheless shows why compression is an incomplete predictor of force. First, local strain determines how far a frame's most strained regions, such as cage hinges or braid crossings, have transformed. The diameter ratio does not fix local strain by itself. If these regions transform across the release window (Figure 2), outward force may change little. If they remain elastic, force rises with each millimeter of compression, as in the linear-elastic model of the Amulet lobe [19]. Provisional engineering interpretation.

Second, a percentage does not account for implant size. Ten to thirty percent of a 40 mm implant is twice the interference represented by the same percentage of a 20 mm implant. If strut section and number stayed constant across sizes, longer struts in the larger frame would bend more easily, and the same percentage would produce less outward force. Public sources do not state whether section and strut count scale with size. Provisional engineering interpretation.

Third, force depends on temperature. Heat treatment sets the austenite-finish temperature (Af), above which the alloy is fully in its high-temperature austenite phase. Both outward and resistive force depend on Af and increase as body temperature rises further above it [20, 22].7 Frames with the same geometry but different heat treatments could therefore meet the same compression criterion and exert different forces. The FDA summaries, US labels and Amulet 360 summary do not state Af. Provisional engineering interpretation.

In the bench study, holding depended more on anchors than on radial force [18]. A simulation study of FLX deployment found a bare-frame model, without fabric or anchors, unstable in 2 of 12 scenarios and concluded that neglecting the anchors "is not acceptable" [23]. In US registry data, implants that migrated or embolized in hospital had a smaller difference between implant size and orifice width (median 4 versus 5 mm) [24]. In this reading, compression affects holding partly through the anchors: how far they engage the wall, and at what angle. Provisional engineering interpretation.

Compression, contact and seal

A peridevice seal requires continuous contact between implant and wall around the full orifice, which is usually oval (Part 1). Flow through the implant fabric is a separate issue. Compression alone does not establish that perimeter contact. In matched comparisons from the Amulet pivotal trial, compression did not differ significantly between patients with severe leaks and leak-free controls in either arm, although the groups were small [25]. In the WATCHMAN 2.5 arm, maximum compression against the native landing zone was 25.6 percent in severe-leak cases and 22.6 percent in matched controls (P = 0.133) [25]. Severe leaks, over 5 mm, after either device occurred mostly with off-axis implants, tilted relative to the appendage axis, or with uncovered appendage lobes [25]. In a CT cohort of Amulet and Amplatzer Cardiac Plug patients, lobe compression alone was associated with a patent (open) appendage. The association was absent in a model that included lobe–appendage alignment; misalignment had a hazard ratio of 38.17 [26]. The 2025 guideline of the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions and the Heart Rhythm Society lists lower compression among risk factors for leak [27]. The 2025 two-center FLX study also found less leak at higher compression [12]. Together, these findings relate compression to seal, while showing that alignment and orifice shape also matter.

The orifice can also change after implantation. In the WATCH-DUAL study of 104 WATCHMAN 2.5 and FLX implants, CT about seven weeks after implant showed that the landing zone had enlarged from 290 to 446 mm² [28]. It had also become rounder. Although all patients met a minimum compression above 10 percent on transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) at release, most were below that level on CT. Leak was detected in 37 percent on CT and 5 percent on TEE at implant; this comparison spans imaging modality and time [28].8 The release check is also made while the delivery cable remains attached [2]. A FLX simulation study found that the attached implant may sit differently after release [29].

Compression is a diameter ratio measured in a few echocardiographic planes on implant day, while the implant remains attached. It samples one determinant of contact at one time point. Provisional engineering interpretation.

Applied to this case, the four framework questions identify implant size relative to the orifice as the adjustable feature. The release compression check is intended to reduce the sensitivity of holding and sealing to fit. A remaining dependency is continuous contact around an orifice that changes after implantation. Evidence of benefit would require follow-up data on leak and device embolization after the orifice has changed. Provisional engineering interpretation.

Validation of computational models

Patient-specific simulation is intended to replace sizing rules with a prediction. Validation to date has focused on geometry, the quantity used by the rules (Table 2). FDA granted the first such software, FEops HEARTguide, a De Novo authorization, FDA's route for novel devices [30]. Before the authorization, the software's prediction of the maximum deployed diameter of WATCHMAN had differed from post-procedural CT by a mean of −1.9 percent in 60 cases [30]. PREDICT-LAA is the only randomized trial of computational simulation found [31]. The primary endpoint was leakage of contrast agent beyond the lobe or device-related thrombus on CT [31]. CT-based simulation for Amulet did not meet this endpoint against standard planning (28.9 versus 41.8 percent, P = 0.08) [31]. Simulation-guided planning increased complete closure and reduced the number of devices and repositionings [31].

Available validations show close prediction of deformation. For individual patients, model predictions have been compared with observed leak in one small study of 30 patients, but not with anchoring (Table 2). PREDICT-LAA tested simulation-guided planning rather than the model's leak predictions. Such predictions also require inputs that remain poorly characterized: the stiffness of an individual appendage wall [29] and the implant's unpublished force curve.

Possible measures for a release criterion: a perspective

Sizing rules appear in sponsor labels. The studies reviewed here suggest possible additional measures at three levels. Author’s perspective, not a prescription.

  • Measurements at release. One candidate is the implant's axis relative to the appendage: severe leaks mostly occurred with off-axis implants, while compression did not differ significantly in matched comparisons [25]. Others are the continuity of contact around the whole perimeter on three-dimensional imaging and, where imaging allows, the number of anchors engaging the wall.
  • Validation at follow-up. Later CT or echocardiography would test whether those measurements predict leak and device embolization after release and landing-zone remodeling.
  • Bench data. These are force–diameter curves for each size, with the frame's Af. They also include the anchor count per unit of contact perimeter, with which pull-out force correlated in one bench comparison of seven systems.

Each candidate would require reproducibility between readers and predictive validation before informing a rule. Public bench curves would also allow clinicians and simulation developers to assess the forces underlying device design.

Summary of findings

  1. The sizing rules are geometric ranges of undisclosed derivation.
  2. One observational study found less leak above 30 percent compression but sets no target.
  3. Regulatory summaries give no radial-force values. No measured force–diameter curves were found for the reviewed WATCHMAN generations or Amulet.
  4. Force at a given compression may also depend on local strain, on how strut sections scale with size (not disclosed) and on Af.
  5. A peridevice seal requires continuous contact. Leak was associated with implant axis and alignment as well as with compression, and the orifice changes after implant.
  6. In the validations available, simulation predicts deformation closely. Its predictions have been compared with observed leak in one small study of 30 patients.

Sustained compression beyond the 10–30 percent range raises a question about fatigue over years of service. Part 4 examines durability through testing, analysis and field experience, including fatigue tests that FDA summaries report only as "Pass".
