Durability: testing, analysis and field experience

Editorial series: Atrial appendage occlusion: four functions, many designs. Part 4 of 4.

Summary. This part examines public evidence on the durability of left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) implants. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) summaries report a "Pass" for 400-million-cycle tests of WATCHMAN 2.5, WATCHMAN FLX and Amulet. They do not disclose the loading or margin to failure. Public field reports document delivery-system recalls, one barb redesign, a suspected manufacturing defect, corrosion and wire breakage found in testing of one device, and isolated frame fractures. These reports establish specific events and responses, but do not provide consistent exposure denominators or establish population failure rates. The part closes with the conclusions of the series.

In this series

  1. Device architectures and the allocation of four functions
  2. Design changes between device generations and their documented outcomes
  3. Sizing, compression and radial force
  4. Durability: testing, analysis and field experience

Introduction

Left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) closes the left atrial appendage, a small pouch of the left atrium where thrombus (blood clot) is often found in patients with atrial fibrillation. It is used in some patients as an alternative to long-term anticoagulation for the prevention of stroke (Part 1). Most implants are self-expanding frames of nitinol, a nickel–titanium alloy, delivered by catheter. This series describes each implant by four functions: seal, hold, heal and deliver. Durability is a requirement under all four. Allocation is the assignment of each function to a component. Coupling means that a change made for one function also changes another. Four questions apply to any design change: which feature becomes independently adjustable, which sensitivity is reduced, which dependency remains, and what evidence would show whether the change helped.

The discussion examines the origin and limits of the 400-million-cycle result, implant loading and frame fatigue, field evidence, surface condition, and regulatory disclosure. It then considers the elements of a durability case before drawing the series conclusions.

Basis of the 400-million-cycle count

FDA's summaries for WATCHMAN 2.5 and WATCHMAN FLX state that implants were "cycled up to the equivalent of 10 years of use or 400,000,000 cycles" [1, 2]. The Amulet summary uses nearly the same words [3]. Each reports the result as "Pass" [1–3].

Four hundred million cycles is ten years at about 76 beats a minute.1 Pelton and colleagues attribute the number to FDA's 1995 stent guidance, under which a stent passed if "fatigue fractures did not occur in 400 million cycles, or an equivalent of 10 years" [4]. FDA's 2010 stent guidance asks for ten years "under pulsatile flow and physiologic loading" but does not contain the number [5]. No occluder summary states why the number was chosen.

Scope of a test-to-success result

Pelton and colleagues call a fixed-cycle pass-or-fail test, such as the 400-million-cycle test, "test-to-success" [4]: a sample set passes if none fractures within a set number of cycles. For nitinol frames of thin struts or wires, a pass supports durability under the applied loading. It does not establish the distance between that loading and failure, or whether the test represents loads in the appendage. Provisional engineering interpretation.

FDA's summaries give the cycle count and result, but omit the test mode, amplitude (displacement or strain), frequency, environment, safety factor and standard. Strain amplitude is half the strain range in a cycle. A safety factor is the ratio of the strain amplitude or load endured to the corresponding service demand. Other definitions of margin are possible, and their terms must be stated. Because these conditions are not reported, the test severity and the margin represented by a pass cannot be determined from the summaries. Provisional engineering interpretation.

Analysis can estimate service strains and compare them with material endurance, while fatigue-to-fracture testing cycles samples above physiologic loading until they fail [6]. The latter identifies failure location and amplitude, but neither method alone establishes a clinical margin. Interpreting a failure amplitude requires a defined clinical demand, a relevant failure mode and a stated statistical treatment. Provisional engineering interpretation.

FDA's stent guidance asks for a fatigue analysis based on mean stress or strain, the average over a cycle, and alternating stress or strain, the amplitude [5].2 FDA's 2021 guidance for devices with nitinol parts asks for a strain-based constant-life curve specific to the final device and its processing [7]. Such a curve shows the combinations of mean and alternating strain that give the same fatigue life. Both guidance documents are non-binding, and whether such analyses were submitted for occluders is not public. The WATCHMAN 2.5 and FLX summaries list a finite element analysis "for characterization", and Amulet's lists none [1–3].

Loading of an implant in the appendage

An occluder's cyclic load is less well defined than a stent's, which is the pulsatile change in an artery's diameter that standard bench tests reproduce [8]. The appendage and its orifice, the opening into the left atrium, contract and relax with the atrium [9]. In a computed tomography study of ten patients scheduled for closure, the orifice's equivalent diameter fell by about 11 percent between relaxation (diastole) and contraction (systole) [9].3 No report located says how much of that motion reaches an implant, or as what strain. Finite-element models of marketed occluders located here simulate crimping (compression into the sheath) and deployment, not years of service [10–13].

Loading also varies among patients and over time.4 In patients with paroxysmal fibrillation, appendage volume at the end of contraction increased during fibrillation but not during sinus rhythm [14]. Tissue covers the implant over weeks to months in animals [15], changing how displacement and force divide between the wall and frame according to their relative stiffness. That stiffness varies among patients and as tissue grows over the implant. A 2025 report (Part 3) found compression above 30 percent in 61 of 236 FLX implants [16]. Greater compression changes the initial strain distribution, but its effect on mean strain or strain amplitude at critical regions depends on local geometry, not overall compression alone. Provisional engineering interpretation.

A durability case is the documented argument that an implant will remain intact for its intended life. Given variation in loading, it should use a justified set of load cases (specified sets of loading conditions) or an envelope rather than one assumed load. The case should distinguish the strain distribution after deployment from cyclic strain changes with rhythm and tissue coverage. It should also address endurance of the metal in its final processed and finished state. Provisional engineering interpretation.

Fatigue of nitinol frames

Nitinol's resistance to fatigue is distinctive but depends on how it is loaded. In electropolished specimens cut from stent tubing, no fractures occurred in ten million cycles below a strain amplitude of about ±0.4 percent [4]. Fatigue life did not fall as mean strain rose to about 1.5 percent [4]. Duerig and colleagues describe superelastic nitinol, which recovers large deformations on unloading, as exceptionally fatigue-resistant where deformation is imposed, and liable to "fatigue rapidly" where force is imposed [17]. These specimen and stent data do not establish an allowable strain, or design limit, for any occluder. An occluder probably experiences both imposed displacement and imposed force, in proportions that are not public and may vary among hinges. Provisional engineering interpretation.

In design analysis, cracks are expected where strain concentrates. In a cut frame, these locations are strut junctions, anchor roots and the heat-affected zones of cuts and welds. In a braid, they are wire crossings, terminations and joints. Where within those regions a crack starts depends on the state of the metal. Shape-setting and heat treatment affect nitinol's transformation temperatures and the stresses at which it transforms. Cold work, inclusions and the surface left by cutting, polishing or coating govern where fatigue cracks start and how much nickel the surface releases. Provisional engineering interpretation.

The metal's processed state also limits how fatigue evidence can be carried between generations. FDA carried earlier WATCHMAN animal data forward to FLX "due to the use of identical materials and substantially similar device designs" (Part 2) [2]. That basis is reasonable for the biological questions addressed, such as tissue response and healing. For fatigue, identical material alone is insufficient because endurance also depends on processing and finish. WATCHMAN approval orders record manufacturing-process changes and new inspections, without stating reasons [18].5 These records document manufacturing controls; they do not establish that a change responded to a field failure or that the evidence bridge was inadequate. In Japan, pulsatile-durability data were resubmitted after changes to WATCHMAN 2.5's suture and filter materials [19]. Provisional engineering interpretation.

Field reports and documented responses

Bench testing evaluates defined conditions, while field reports describe observed events and any documented responses. Figure 1 locates those responses by function.

Delivery-system problems and design responses. Documented responses to delivery problems included a reinforced WATCHMAN cable [20] and a WaveCrest sheath-liner change [21]. A delivery cable of the Amplatzer Cardiac Plug (ACP) fractured in torsion, prompting new instructions [22]. In the United States (US) recall database, every Class 1 recall (FDA's most serious category) of a WATCHMAN, Amplatzer or WaveCrest product concerns the delivery system or access hardware [23–26]. None is a recall of a WATCHMAN, FLX, FLX Pro, ACP or Amulet implant [23–26].6

WATCHMAN barb damage and redesign. A barb is the implant's type of anchor. Among reviewed sponsor and regulator documents, the only implant redesign linked to an observed fracture was the 2006 WATCHMAN case (Part 1) [27, 28].

Ultraseal fractures and manufacturing concerns. In one series, 2 of 18 patients had fractures of the jointed bulb-and-sail Ultraseal I device [29]. Both discs embolized (detached into the bloodstream) [29]. The sponsor's 2020 notice reported 3 fractures among 750 devices and stated that "a manufacturing defect may be present" in some initial lots [30]. One fracture had not been detected at the 6- and 12-month examinations [30].7 At echocardiographic follow-up (cardiac ultrasound; median 79 days), investigators observed no fractures in the redesigned Ultraseal II and wrote that design changes "may likely have contributed" [31]. These reports have different follow-up periods and denominators, so their counts do not by themselves establish a comparative failure rate or a causal effect of redesign.

OMEGA corrosion and wire breakage in testing. In November 2024 Eclipse Medical recalled every unused OMEGA occluder, a platinum-coated device [32].8 The recall followed testing to support certification under the European Union (EU) Medical Device Regulation [32]. That testing, "to verify fatigue and durability, as well as metallic ion release", gave "unsatisfactory results, specifically related to excessive Nickel leaching caused by corrosion, and wire breakage" [32]. The notice did not say which test produced which result. This recall documents corrosion and wire breakage detected during durability and metallic-ion testing of one device. It does not estimate how often these failures occur across the device class.

Case reports and surveillance limits. A single-center research letter reported central-junction fractures in 2 of 891 LAmbre patients [33]. One fracture was identified at three years after two surveillance echocardiograms had not detected it [33]. Single-case meeting abstracts describe strut fractures with leak through WATCHMAN and FLX Pro frames.9 These reports identify possible events and limits of surveillance imaging; they do not provide population rates, since exposure and follow-up are not consistently characterized.

In several documented cases reviewed here, failures involved features associated with a function allocation. Examples include barbs passed through the sheath and the junction between LAmbre's umbrella and cover. Documented responses include redesign, withdrawal of affected serial numbers, recall, and review of final inspection after an Amulet lobe wire was found broken during preparation [34]. The sources do not establish whether these responses addressed the causes, and they cannot identify responses that were not made public. Provisional engineering interpretation.

The four questions also apply to changes at these features. As described in Part 2, Amulet 360's anchors were reshaped to allow full recapture, drawing a deployed implant back into its sheath before release [35]. This is a change to anchor geometry, rather than evidence that fixation became independently adjustable. Each anchor must still pass through the sheath, as the WATCHMAN barbs did in 2006. Anchor integrity after label-permitted recaptures would be relevant evidence of durability. Provisional engineering interpretation.

Surface condition, corrosion and nickel release

The implant surface serves the heal function (Part 2), and its condition also affects fatigue-crack initiation and nickel release. Provisional engineering interpretation.

LAAO devices use several finishes: electropolished nitinol in WATCHMAN [1], a titanium nitride covering in LAmbre [36], platinum-coated mesh in OMEGA [32], and a fluoropolymer coating in FLX Pro, poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP) [37–39].10 Whether the FLX Pro coating bears on the frame's fatigue or nickel release depends on whether it covers the metal, which the documents describe differently. Current devices also combine metals.11

FDA's 2021 guidance makes four requests on corrosion and nickel release [7]:

  • pitting tests, for corrosion that forms small local cavities, after simulated implantation;
  • nickel release over 60 days where a device fails the pitting tests or lacks an established finish such as electropolish;
  • galvanic testing, for corrosion caused by contact between different metals, to be considered where metals differ;
  • uncoated tests of polymer-coated devices.

The occluder documents report a Pass for FLX's nickel elution test [2] and "acceptable nickel levels" for Amulet [3]. Amulet's US label states that nickel is released for at least 120 days [40]. Amulet 360's EU summary states at least 60 days [35]. None of the four documents gives the amount of nickel released [2, 3, 35, 40].

Durability information in public regulatory documents

Disclosure differs by function and jurisdiction (Table 1).12 The review report of Japan's Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) for WATCHMAN 2.5 names radial-force, dislodgement and pulsatile-durability tests without values [19, 41]. Radial force is the force between the implant and the wall: the outward force the implant exerts as it expands, or the force with which it resists compression. Four quantities are absent from every FDA, PMDA and EU document read: radial-force values, anchor-strength values, corrosion or nickel-release numbers, and the fatigue loading.

A durability case: a perspective

The reviewed evidence suggests six elements that would make a public durability assessment more interpretable. Author’s perspective, not a prescription.

  • A justified set of load cases. Appendage motion from imaging, converted to displacement at the implant, by rhythm and before and after the implant is covered, at the worst-case size and at the compressions now reported.
  • A strain analysis. Finite-element strain at hinges, anchor roots, junctions and wire terminations under those load cases, with the frame's transformation temperature, compared with a strain-based constant-life curve for the finished device.
  • Margin, with its terms stated. Test-to-success testing, analysis and, where suitable, fatigue-to-fracture testing, presented together, with the terms of any margin stated.
  • Recapture as a load case. Integrity after the recaptures that the label allows, tested in a way modeled on WATCHMAN 2.5's barb "cleat test", which deflects a barb to the strain of recapture [1].
  • Surface condition as part of fatigue. Corrosion, pitting and nickel release after simulated recapture, on coated devices and their uncoated worst case, with galvanic tests where metals differ.
  • Follow-up imaging that can detect a fracture. The Ultraseal and LAmbre reports describe fractures that routine follow-up imaging did not show [30, 33].

FDA's stent and nitinol guidance already requests much of this evidence. A public summary of load cases, strain limits and margin definitions would make a reported pass interpretable.

Conclusions of the series

Across regulatory documents, patents, registries, field notices and published reports, the series examined the engineering design of LAAO implants. Its findings are summarized by part below.

  1. In the United States and Japan, every LAAO implant approved for use inside the heart is a cage or a lobe and disc. No architecture reviewed makes the four functions independent (Part 1).
  2. Where outcomes were measured, some generations recorded better outcomes than predecessors. Public documents do not attribute the FLX registry or Amulet 360 single-arm results to a single design change (Part 2).
  3. Sizing rules specify geometric ranges of undisclosed derivation. No measured force–diameter curves were found for WATCHMAN 2.5, FLX, FLX Pro or Amulet in the public literature reviewed (Part 3).
  4. FDA summaries report a pass in a 400-million-cycle fatigue test without reporting its loading or margin. The author's engineering reading is that some documented failures involved features associated with an allocation. Figure 1 groups observed problems by function and shows where documented responses occurred (Part 4).

These findings depend on four distinctions.

  1. Architecture versus fabrication. How a design allocates the four functions is a separate choice from whether it is cut or braided (Part 1).
  2. Holding versus radial force. In one bench study of seven systems, pull-out force correlated with anchor density (r = 0.87). The authors concluded that stability depends more on anchoring structures than on radial force (Part 3). Changes made for holding also alter where and how the wall is loaded (Part 2).
  3. Perimeter contact versus a diameter ratio. A seal requires continuous contact around a changing orifice, which a diameter percentage measures only in part (Part 3).
  4. Endurance versus margin. A test-to-success result establishes endurance under specified conditions. A durability margin requires stated loads and stated terms (Part 4).

The public evidence answers the four design questions only in part. One independently adjustable feature is a seal checked before fixation. Another is a disc sealing at the orifice while a lobe holds deeper in the appendage, although the lobe also seals (Part 1). Amulet 360's European summary gives a reason for each change and addresses more questions than documents for most generations. Evidence for the fourth question remains partial (Part 2). Similarly, fatigue summaries report a pass without loading conditions, so they do not support a judgment about design margin.

This review uses public documents available through September 2026. The allocations and engineering readings are the author's interpretations. Japanese documents were read in the original and translated for this series. Device selection follows Table 1 of Part 1.

Public reporting could support comparison by stating, for each design change, the function it serves and the other functions it affects, as the Amulet 360 summary does in part. For each device, it could also report the mechanical quantities underlying its claims: radial-force curves by size, anchor strength, fatigue load cases and margin, and nickel release. Author’s perspective, not a prescription.
